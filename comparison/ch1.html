Distinction Between Bayesian and Frequentist Paradigms

Comparison of key aspects:

Aspect Bayesian (Subjectivist) Frequentist (Classical / Objective)
Source of Information Combines all available information, including prior beliefs and observed data. Uses only objective data obtained from the sampling process; discards subjective information.
View of Probability Probability represents degree of belief or subjective uncertainty about parameters. Probability represents long-run frequency of events in repeated sampling.
Treatment of Parameters Parameters are random variables with prior distributions reflecting uncertainty. Parameters are fixed but unknown constants; randomness comes only from the data.
Goal of Inference Update beliefs about parameters using Bayes’ theorem → obtain posterior distribution. Construct estimators, confidence intervals, and tests based on the sampling distribution.
Type of Information Retained All available information (prior knowledge + data). Objective sample information only.
Interpretation of Uncertainty Quantified through the posterior probability of parameters or hypotheses. Quantified through sampling variability (e.g., standard error, p-values).
Example Question “Given the data, what is the probability that \( \theta \) lies in this range?” “If we repeat the experiment many times, how often will our estimator be within this range of \( \theta \)?”

Visualization (Conceptual Picture)

  • Bayesian: Starts with a prior belief → collects data → updates to posterior.

(Prior → Data (Update) → Posterior)

  • Frequentist: Focuses on data-driven inference → repeats sampling mentally to understand variability.

(Data → Sampling Distribution (Inference) → Estimator / Confidence Interval)

Summary / Insight

The two paradigms differ fundamentally in what they treat as “information” and how they quantify uncertainty. Bayesian methods explicitly incorporate prior knowledge and provide probability statements about parameters; frequentist methods emphasize procedures with guaranteed long-run properties based only on the sampling process. Understanding these contrasts helps choose the right approach for a problem and to communicate clearly what assumptions and sources of information informed the analysis.

Distinction Between the Three Interpretations of Probability

Comparison table of key aspects:

Aspect Physical / Classical Frequentist Subjective (Bayesian)
Core Idea Probability is the ratio of favorable outcomes to total equally likely outcomes. Probability is the long-run relative frequency of an event in repeated independent trials. Probability is a degree of belief in an event, given available information.
Formal Definition \(P(A) = \dfrac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total possible outcomes}}\) \(P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{m}{n}\), where \(m\) = number of occurrences of \(A\) in \(n\) trials. \(P(A \mid H)\): probability of \(A\) given background information \(H\).
Type of Information Used Based on a priori reasoning and symmetry (e.g., dice, coins, cards). Based purely on empirical data and repeatable experiments. Based on all available knowledge, including subjective and contextual information.
Interpretation of Probability Objective and mathematical — assumes equal likelihood of elementary outcomes. Objective and empirical — probability is an observable property of a repeatable experiment. Subjective — probability quantifies personal uncertainty or belief.
Applicability Works well in games of chance and finite sample spaces. Works well for repeatable random experiments. Works for unique or non-repeatable events (e.g., rain tomorrow, patient has disease).
Main Limitations
  • Requires assumption of equally likely outcomes.
  • Can involve circular reasoning in defining “chance.”
  • True limit \(n \to \infty\) never observed.
  • Requires notion of identical, independent trials.
  • Depends on personal judgment and available information.
  • Can vary from person to person.
Example Probability of getting heads = \(1/2\) when tossing a fair coin. Probability of rain = proportion of rainy days in a long sequence of similar days. Probability of rain = degree of belief, updated with weather reports or personal experience.
Key Figure / Philosophy Laplace’s classical probability. von Mises, Fisher, and the frequentist school. De Finetti, Savage, and the Bayesian school.

Conceptual Summary

Classical: probability from symmetry → good for dice, cards, and finite sample spaces.

Frequentist: probability from repetition → good for physical experiments and long-run properties.

Subjective (Bayesian): probability from beliefs and information → well-suited for unique events and inference where prior knowledge matters.

Illustrative Example — Classical vs Bayesian Reasoning

1. The Baby Gender Problem Setup

Suppose a couple has six children, with five boys and one girl. We wish to infer the probability \( \theta \) that a baby is male.

2. Frequentist (Classical) Analysis

  • \( X_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\theta) \), \( i = 1, \dots, 6 \).
  • Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE): \( \hat{\theta} = \frac{5}{6} = 0.833 \).
  • Testing \( H_0: \theta = 0.5 \):
    • If data collected for fixed \( n=6 \): \( P(Y \ge 5) = 0.109 \).
    • If data collected until first girl: \( P(Z \ge 5) = 0.031 \).
    • Thus, inference depends on the stopping rule.

The frequentist inference relies solely on the data and is sensitive to the sampling plan.

3. Role of Stopping Rules

In frequentist reasoning, the stopping rule affects the distribution under \( H_0 \), and hence the p-value. This leads to paradoxes when comparing different experimental designs producing the same observed data.

4. Bayesian Analysis with Prior and Posterior Updating

  • Priors: \( \theta \sim \text{Beta}(a, b) \).
  • Likelihood: \( X_1, \dots, X_6 \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\theta) \).
  • Posterior: \( \theta \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(a + 5, b + 1) \).
  • Example: With prior \( \text{Beta}(2,2) \), posterior \( \text{Beta}(7,3) \).
  • Posterior mean: \( E[\theta \mid \text{data}] = \frac{a+5}{a+b+6} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7 \).
  • Predictive probability that the next baby is male = 0.70.

The Bayesian result combines prior belief with data and is invariant to the stopping rule.

5. Interpretation and Comparison

AspectFrequentist (Classical)Bayesian (Subjective)
Unknown Quantity\( \theta \) is fixed but unknown.\( \theta \) is random with a prior distribution.
Data UsedOnly observed outcomes (5 boys, 1 girl).Observed outcomes + prior information.
Estimator\( \hat{\theta} = 0.833 \).Posterior mean \( E[\theta \mid \text{data}] = 0.70 \).
Effect of Stopping RuleInference depends on sampling plan.Inference depends only on observed data (exchangeability).
InterpretationProbability = long-run frequency.Probability = degree of belief updated by evidence.

6. Conceptual Visualization

Imagine a tug-of-war between two philosophies:

  • Frequentist: “Let the data alone speak.” (Estimate \( 0.83 \) directly from 5 boys.)
  • Bayesian: “Combine prior belief with data.” (Prior \( \text{Beta}(2,2) \) leads to posterior mean \( 0.70 \).)

7. Interpretive Summary

  • Frequentist: Parameters are fixed; uncertainty arises from data.
  • Bayesian: Parameters are random; uncertainty updates with evidence.
  • Stopping rules affect frequentist inference but not Bayesian inference.
  • Bayesian reasoning offers coherent, predictive interpretation via posterior distribution.

Common Statistical Models

1. Random Sampling Model

  • \( X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n \) are iid with common density \( f(x; \theta) \).
  • Parameter \( \theta \) represents unknown characteristics of the population.
  • Represents the simplest and most general data-generation framework.

Example: Sampling the heights of students to estimate the mean height.

🎲 Each observation is an independent draw from the same population curve \( f(x; \theta) \).

2. Location Model

  • Model: \( X = \theta + \varepsilon \), where \( \varepsilon \sim f(e) \).
  • Parameter \( \theta \) is the location parameter.
  • Shifts the entire distribution horizontally without altering its shape.

Example: Measurement error model \( X_i = \theta + e_i \).

📉 The distribution slides left or right as \( \theta \) changes, but shape remains the same.

3. Scale Model

  • Model: \( X = \sigma \varepsilon \), where \( \varepsilon \sim f(e) \).
  • Parameter \( \sigma > 0 \) is the scale parameter.
  • Controls spread or variability — affects width but not location.

Example: Time-to-failure scaled by device quality \( X_i = \sigma e_i \).

🔍 Larger \( \sigma \): wider spread; smaller \( \sigma \): narrower curve centered at 0.

4. Location–Scale Model

  • Model: \( X = \theta + \sigma \varepsilon \), where \( \varepsilon \sim f(e) \).
  • Parameters: \( \theta \) (location), \( \sigma \) (scale).
  • Combines both translation and scaling effects.

Example: \( X_i \sim N(\theta, \sigma^2) \).

Moves and resizes the same “template” density \( f(e) \) to match the observed data’s center and spread.

5. Example — Measurement Error Model

Setup: \( X_i = \theta + e_i, \; i = 1, \dots, n \), where

  • \( \theta \): true value of the measured quantity (unknown),
  • \( e_i \): random measurement errors, iid with distribution \( F_e \).

Joint Density:

\[ p(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f_e(x_i - \theta) \]

This shows that the data depend on \( \theta \) through a simple horizontal shift — hence, a location model.

6. Role of Probability Density and Independence

  • The density \( f(x; \theta) \) connects observed data to model parameters.
  • Independence ensures each observation contributes equally to inference.
  • This simplifies computation and clarifies the contribution of each data point.

7. Intuitive Visualization

  • Location model: Bell curve slides left/right — same shape.
  • Scale model: Bell curve stretches or shrinks — same center.
  • Location–scale model: Bell both shifts and stretches — most general form.

8. Conceptual Summary

  • These models form the building blocks of statistical inference.
  • The iid assumption ensures uniform contribution of all data points.
  • Understanding how location and scale parameters affect distributions is key to estimation, testing, and Bayesian analysis.

Bayes’ Theorem — Big Picture

Bayes’ theorem is a mechanical rule for updating probabilities about unknown quantities (parameters, hypotheses) when new data arrive. It converts a prior belief about a parameter (\(\theta\)) into a posterior belief (\(\pi(\theta \mid x)\)) by combining the prior with the data likelihood. The posterior then drives prediction, decision-making, model-checking, and sequential updates. All Bayesian inference—prediction, conjugacy, sequential updating, model-checking—flows from this algebraic identity.

1. Formula and Interpretation

Given:

  • Parameter \(\theta\) in \(\Theta\)
  • Prior \(\pi(\theta)\): belief about \(\theta\) before seeing data
  • Sampling model \(p(x\mid\theta)\): likelihood of observing \(x\) given \(\theta\)

Bayes’ theorem:

\[ \pi(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta) \pi(\theta)}{p(x)}, \quad p(x) = \int p(x \mid \theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta \]

  • Numerator: prior × likelihood
  • Denominator: normalization constant (prior predictive)
  • Posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid x)\) is the updated belief

Useful forms:

  • Unnormalized posterior: \(\pi(\theta \mid x) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\pi(\theta)\)
  • Predictive for future \(Y\): \(p(y\mid x) = \int p(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta\)

2. Discrete vs Continuous

Same logic applies: multiply prior by likelihood, divide by sum/integral over \(\theta\). Denominator is just a normalizing constant.

3. Posterior Predictive Distribution

For future observation \(Y\):

\[ p(y \mid x) = \int p(y \mid \theta)\pi(\theta \mid x)\,d\theta = \mathbb{E}_{\pi(\cdot \mid x)}[p(y \mid \theta)] \]

Prediction = average of model predictions over the posterior. Provides a natural way to check model adequacy.

4. Sequential Updating and Commutativity

With conditionally independent data \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\):

  • After \(X_1\): \(\pi(\theta \mid x_1) \propto p(x_1 \mid \theta)\pi(\theta)\)
  • After \(X_2\): \(\pi(\theta \mid x_1,x_2) \propto p(x_2\mid\theta)\pi(\theta \mid x_1)\)

Order does not matter; final posterior depends only on observed data, ensuring sequential coherence.

5. Prior Predictive for Model Checking

\(p(x) = \int p(x\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)d\theta\) is the prior predictive. Tiny \(p(x)\) suggests prior/model mismatch or outlier.

6. Toy Example: Doctor and Two Tests

Setup:

  • \(\theta \in \{0,1\}\), disease present if \(\theta=1\)
  • Prior: \(P(\theta=1)=0.70\), \(P(\theta=0)=0.30\)
  • Test X: sensitivity 0.95, false positive 0.40
  • Observed \(X=1\)

Posterior:

\[ P(\theta=1\mid X=1) = \frac{0.95 \cdot 0.70}{0.95 \cdot 0.70 + 0.40 \cdot 0.30} \approx 0.847 \]

Posterior predictive for second test \(Y\):

\[ P(Y=1\mid X=1) = 0.99 \cdot 0.8466 + 0.004 \cdot 0.1534 \approx 0.839 \]

Update after \(Y=0\): \(P(\theta=1\mid X=1,Y=0) \approx 0.053\)

Lesson: posterior after X becomes prior for Y; multiply by new likelihood and renormalize.

7. Conjugacy Example: Normal Prior + Normal Sampling

  • Prior: \(\theta \sim N(\mu_0,\tau_0^2)\)
  • Likelihood: \(X \mid \theta \sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)\)

Posterior:

\[ \tau_1^2 = \frac{1}{\tau_0^{-2} + \sigma^{-2}}, \quad \mu_1 = \tau_1^2(\tau_0^{-2}\mu_0 + \sigma^{-2}x) \]

Equivalently (precision notation \(\lambda_0 = \tau_0^{-2}, \lambda = \sigma^{-2}\)):

\[ \lambda_1 = \lambda_0 + \lambda, \quad \mu_1 = \frac{\lambda_0 \mu_0 + \lambda x}{\lambda_0 + \lambda} \]

Posterior mean = weighted average of prior mean and data, weights = precisions (information).

8. Example: Box & Tiao

Observation: \(X=850\)

  • Physicist A: prior \(N(900,20^2)\), posterior mean ≈ 890, sd ≈ 17.9
  • Physicist B: prior \(N(800,80^2)\), posterior mean ≈ 840, sd ≈ 35.8

Interpretation: Strong prior → modest update; weak prior → large update.

9. Exchangeability Remark

Observations are exchangeable if the joint distribution depends only on counts, not order. Posterior depends on counts, not stopping rule or order.

10. Key Practical Points

  • Posterior summarizes all info from prior and data.
  • Prior choice matters, especially with weak data.
  • Use prior predictive for model plausibility; posterior predictive for checking data fit.
  • Sequential coherence ensures order of processing doesn’t matter.
  • Conjugate priors provide closed-form posteriors; non-conjugate requires numerical methods (MCMC, Laplace, VI).
  • Predictive probabilities are testable; posterior probabilities for parameters are not directly observable.

Compact Takeaway

Bayes’ theorem: “Update beliefs by multiplying prior by likelihood, then renormalize.” This produces posterior distributions, predictive distributions, sequential coherence, and a transparent accounting of information when conjugacy holds.
