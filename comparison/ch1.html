Distinction Between Bayesian and Frequentist Paradigms

Comparison of key aspects:

Aspect Bayesian (Subjectivist) Frequentist (Classical / Objective)
Source of Information Combines all available information, including prior beliefs and observed data. Uses only objective data obtained from the sampling process; discards subjective information.
View of Probability Probability represents degree of belief or subjective uncertainty about parameters. Probability represents long-run frequency of events in repeated sampling.
Treatment of Parameters Parameters are random variables with prior distributions reflecting uncertainty. Parameters are fixed but unknown constants; randomness comes only from the data.
Goal of Inference Update beliefs about parameters using Bayes’ theorem → obtain posterior distribution. Construct estimators, confidence intervals, and tests based on the sampling distribution.
Type of Information Retained All available information (prior knowledge + data). Objective sample information only.
Interpretation of Uncertainty Quantified through the posterior probability of parameters or hypotheses. Quantified through sampling variability (e.g., standard error, p-values).
Example Question “Given the data, what is the probability that \( \theta \) lies in this range?” “If we repeat the experiment many times, how often will our estimator be within this range of \( \theta \)?”

Summary / Insight

The two paradigms differ fundamentally in what they treat as “information” and how they quantify uncertainty. Bayesian methods explicitly incorporate prior knowledge and provide probability statements about parameters; frequentist methods emphasize procedures with guaranteed long-run properties based only on the sampling process. Understanding these contrasts helps choose the right approach for a problem and to communicate clearly what assumptions and sources of information informed the analysis.

Distinction Between the Three Interpretations of Probability

Comparison table of key aspects:

Aspect Physical / Classical Frequentist Subjective (Bayesian)
Core Idea Probability is the ratio of favorable outcomes to total equally likely outcomes. Probability is the long-run relative frequency of an event in repeated independent trials. Probability is a degree of belief in an event, given available information.
Formal Definition \(P(A) = \dfrac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total possible outcomes}}\) \(P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{m}{n}\), where \(m\) = number of occurrences of \(A\) in \(n\) trials. \(P(A \mid H)\): probability of \(A\) given background information \(H\).
Type of Information Used Based on a priori reasoning and symmetry (e.g., dice, coins, cards). Based purely on empirical data and repeatable experiments. Based on all available knowledge, including subjective and contextual information.
Interpretation of Probability Objective and mathematical — assumes equal likelihood of elementary outcomes. Objective and empirical — probability is an observable property of a repeatable experiment. Subjective — probability quantifies personal uncertainty or belief.
Applicability Works well in games of chance and finite sample spaces. Works well for repeatable random experiments. Works for unique or non-repeatable events (e.g., rain tomorrow, patient has disease).
Main Limitations
  • Requires assumption of equally likely outcomes.
  • Can involve circular reasoning in defining “chance.”
  • True limit \(n \to \infty\) never observed.
  • Requires notion of identical, independent trials.
  • Depends on personal judgment and available information.
  • Can vary from person to person.
Example Probability of getting heads = \(1/2\) when tossing a fair coin. Probability of rain = proportion of rainy days in a long sequence of similar days. Probability of rain = degree of belief, updated with weather reports or personal experience.
Key Figure / Philosophy Laplace’s classical probability. von Mises, Fisher, and the frequentist school. De Finetti, Savage, and the Bayesian school.

Conceptual Summary

Classical: probability from symmetry → good for dice, cards, and finite sample spaces.

Frequentist: probability from repetition → good for physical experiments and long-run properties.

Subjective (Bayesian): probability from beliefs and information → well-suited for unique events and inference where prior knowledge matters.
