Distinction Between Bayesian and Frequentist Paradigms

Comparison of key aspects:

Aspect Bayesian (Subjectivist) Frequentist (Classical / Objective)
Source of Information Combines all available information, including prior beliefs and observed data. Uses only objective data obtained from the sampling process; discards subjective information.
View of Probability Probability represents degree of belief or subjective uncertainty about parameters. Probability represents long-run frequency of events in repeated sampling.
Treatment of Parameters Parameters are random variables with prior distributions reflecting uncertainty. Parameters are fixed but unknown constants; randomness comes only from the data.
Goal of Inference Update beliefs about parameters using Bayes’ theorem → obtain posterior distribution. Construct estimators, confidence intervals, and tests based on the sampling distribution.
Type of Information Retained All available information (prior knowledge + data). Objective sample information only.
Interpretation of Uncertainty Quantified through the posterior probability of parameters or hypotheses. Quantified through sampling variability (e.g., standard error, p-values).
Example Question “Given the data, what is the probability that \( \theta \) lies in this range?” “If we repeat the experiment many times, how often will our estimator be within this range of \( \theta \)?”

Summary / Insight

The two paradigms differ fundamentally in what they treat as “information” and how they quantify uncertainty. Bayesian methods explicitly incorporate prior knowledge and provide probability statements about parameters; frequentist methods emphasize procedures with guaranteed long-run properties based only on the sampling process. Understanding these contrasts helps choose the right approach for a problem and to communicate clearly what assumptions and sources of information informed the analysis.
